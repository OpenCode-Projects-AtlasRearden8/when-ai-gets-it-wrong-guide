# High-Stakes vs. Low-Stakes Asks

Chapter 02 gave you the taxonomy of mistakes. This chapter gives you the
one question that decides how much checking any answer deserves. You will
ask an AI a hundred things this month; checking everything exhaustively
would eat your life, and checking nothing would eventually bite. The trick
is triage, and the whole test is one sentence:

**If this answer is wrong, who pays?**

## The stakes test

Run every ask through one invisible question before you even click send:

1. **No one pays** — it's brainstorming, drafting, curiosity, a first
   attempt at something you'll shape yourself. *Skim and use.*
2. **You pay a little** — a wrong fact costs you minutes to discover and
   correct: a recipe that's slightly off, a mildly wrong historical date
   in a hobby project. *Skim; spot-check anything numeric.*
3. **You pay a lot** — a wrong fact costs real money, real health, real
   legal trouble, or a relationship. *Check before you trust — chapter 04
   is for these.*
4. **Someone else pays, or it's permanent** — anything affecting another
   person's health, money, rights, or record; anything that goes in an
   official file. *Never delegate — chapter 07 territory. AI may help you
   organize; it never gets the final call.*

The beautiful thing about this test is that it's *about you*, not about
the answer. The same AI question asked on Tuesday and at tax time can land
in different rows — the stakes are in your life, not in the model.

## What lands in each row

### Row 1: skim and use

- Brainstorming names, titles, ideas, birthday themes
- First-draft text of anything you will personalize anyway
- Explaining a concept at a level you'll re-explain to yourself
- "Give me ten angles on this," "what should I ask the mechanic?"
- Travel *ideas* (not bookings), gift ideas, practice interview questions

None of this means the answer is right. It means being wrong is cheap.
Enjoy your tool at full speed here — this is where AI is most fun, and
soft answers are the reason the hard ones are worth catching.

### Row 2: skim, with a numbers pass

- Recipes and how-tos you'll adjust while following
- Summaries of things you have the original of (a meeting you attended, a
  document in front of you)
- Estimates you'll round generously either direction
- General background: "how do eyeglasses work," "what way should ceiling
  fans spin in summer"

The numbers pass: any count, total, or measure in a row-2 answer gets two
seconds of squinting, because numbers are precision gaps (chapter 01) even
in otherwise-fine answers.

### Row 3: verify before you trust

- Facts you'll repeat to other people (in a report, a talk, the group chat)
- Anything involving money terms: fees, rates, totals, refund policies
- Software steps you'll follow in something important
- Health-adjacent general info *before it meets a doctor* — background
  for the appointment, never a verdict
- Deadlines, dates, and "how many days until" math
- Citations, quotes, or sources you'll use anywhere

These answers are where the verify-first habit lives. It's not much time —
the load-bearing details are usually one to four items, and chapter 04
shows how to find them in a minute.

### Row 4: AI never gets the final call

- Law, medicine, money — the three big ones, unpacked in chapter 07
- Government forms, official applications, identity documents
- Instructions to another person about their health or safety
- Anything a licensed or regulated human will later be paid to double-check
- Giving someone else an AI-written answer *as your own confident word* on
  one of these subjects

In row 4, AI is still useful — as an explainer, a translator, an
organizer, and a question-writer for the professional you'll actually
consult. What it never is in row 4: the last word.

## The gray zones (and how to read them)

| Situation | Why it's gray | The resolution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A health question for yourself, mild-sounding | "For myself" feels low-stakes; medicine doesn't care | Row 3 for information; the *decision* (treat/stop/wait) is always row 4 |
| A small purchase decision | Money, but small | Row 2 — unless the fee/total math is load-bearing, then row 3 |
| Homework citation | Low personal stakes, high integrity stakes | Row 3 — an invented citation (mistake #4) is checked every time, no exceptions |
| Advice you'll forward to a friend | Their stakes, your credibility | Row 3 — you're now the source, so you own the checking |
| "Is this email too harsh?" | Zero facts, high feel | Row 1 — but ask neutrally, or sycophancy (mistake #8) will pat your hand |

## Two habits that make the test stick

**Say the row out loud.** Not the whole chant — just mutter "row three"
before you ask, when it's row three. Two weeks of this and the sorting
becomes instant, the same way you glance at a pan handle before grabbing
it forever after one burn.

**Default anything *you* will repeat to row 3.** If you're going to repeat
it in a meeting, a message, a paper, or a conversation with someone who
will act on it, that handoff makes it load-bearing right there. You
became the citation. Chapter 04 shows how to earn that.

:::note The test is about who pays, not how confident it sounds
Notice what the test never includes: the tone of the answer. A row-3
answer delivered in a warm, structured, beautifully formatted way is still
row 3. Formatting is free; confidence is a register, not knowledge
(chapter 01). The stakes live in your life, and only you know them.
:::
