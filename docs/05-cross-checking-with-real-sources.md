# Cross-Checking With Real Sources

The verification loop from chapter 04 needs *somewhere* for the check to
land: a source that actually exists, that actually addresses your
question, and that actually says what was claimed. This chapter is about
how to find and use those sources; getting the check out of the AI's
hands and into the real world's.

The core principle, and the spine of this whole guide:

> **The sources you can reach are worth more than the details the model
> can recite.** You are the bridge between the two.

## The ranking of evidence

Not all "I googled it" checks are equal. From strongest to weakest for our
purpose:

1. **The primary source** — the actual document: the government page for
   the form, the product's own manual for the procedure, the publisher's
   catalog entry for the book, the hospital's own drug sheet. When a
   primary source exists, nothing beats it.
2. **A second independent write-up** — a reputable article, a textbook, a
   specialist site, that separately reports the same fact. Two
   independent reports agreeing isn't proof, but it moves a fact from
   "trust" to "solid."
3. **The user community for that thing** — the subreddit, forum, or
   product Q&A where people who live with this every day describe it.
   Excellent for procedure drift (mistake #10), because the answers
   usually date themselves when the software changes.
4. **The AI's own word again** — worth least of all. "Are you sure?" and
   a second answer from the *same* model are chapters 01 and 02's
   uncertainty blindness in action, not evidence.

When you get to a real source, read the *parts you actually need*. Every
sort of source has a shelf where the specific answer lives.

## The five checks, with the exact search move

Each kind of load-bearing detail has a go-to move. None takes more than a
minute or two; the exact-quote search takes seconds.

### 1. Facts and names → the landing-page check

Search the fact in ordinary words, then prefer the official destination:
the manufacturer's page, the state's page, the hospital's page. Read only the sentence that answers your question. If the official
page says something different from the model, the model is wrong full
stop — no weighing of "which one sounds more right."

### 2. Quotes and citations → the exact-phrase search

Copy the quoted words — the claimed sentence, the paper title, the case
name — wrap the whole thing in **quotation marks** and search it. Exact
matches for a real, claimed quote should turn up the primary source. If
the exact phrase exists nowhere on the internet, the quote or citation is
almost certainly fabricated (mistake #4 or #5). This is
double-hatted: it is also the manual's check for fake screenshots, and
its sibling lives in *The Spot Fake Media Guide*.

### 3. Numbers and dates → your own arithmetic

Do the count yourself. Open the calculator app for anything with three or
more digits; count list items by pointing at the screen; subtract the
years like a human. This one has no substitute: a model's arithmetic can
be recomputed by it to different results, so *it must be recomputed by
you*. If the number matters for money or medicine — the calculator plus
the current account balance, the pharmacist rechecking the dose — then
your check layers on top of a professional's, it doesn't replace one.

### 4. Software procedures → trust the screen

Open the actual app and follow the steps *against reality*, reading the
menus as they are. Procedure drift (mistake #10) means the model's
described screen can be a few versions out of date. The product's real
help pages or a recent community thread are your tiebreakers when the
steps and the screen can't be reconciled.

### 5. Time-sensitive anything → the source's own date label

For anything with a "current" — prices, regulations, hours, deadlines —
find the page's own timestamp and prefer source dates over the model's
memory. When the source has no date anywhere, treat it as fragile and
look once more.

## The three-source habit (for row 3 answers you'll repeat)

For the facts you're going to repeat in a meeting, a paper, or the group
chat, give yourself this simple bar:

| Number of independent sources | Verdict |
| --- | --- |
| 1 (the AI) | interesting, unverified — fine for you, not for others |
| 2 (the AI + one source) | good enough for low personal stakes |
| 3 (the AI + two genuinely independent sources) | safe to repeat as your own word |
| sources that contradict each other | unresolved — say so, or dig one level deeper |

"Independent" is doing real work in that table. Two articles both citing
the same press release is *one* source wearing two outfits. Reach for a
little diversity at the top level and you're covered.

:::warn A dead form of checking: the confident link
AI tools will often hand you URLs. A link is not a source until it loads
and actually says it; hallucinated links (mistake #4's cousin) are
real-looking. Click it once — that thirty seconds is the whole difference
between "the model gave me a source" and "you have a source." A dead or
redirected link is itself useful data: it usually means the topic has
moved on since the model's training froze.
:::

## Asking the AI to help with the check (before it becomes the check)

There's a decent use of the model *inside* this chapter: turning its
answer into a verification plan. Ask for:

- "What search phrases would I use to confirm the claim about X?"
- "What kind of source would be the authority on this, and where would I
  find its current version?"

You're using the AI as a librarian, not an oracle: it's excellent at
describing the shape of a good evidence trail — its best ability, and the
part least harmed by its failures — and you do the verification.
Chapter 08 of *The Spot Fake Media Guide* has the parallel skill for
media fakes — asking AI to help check the media, with the same trust
boundary.

## The calm summary

- Primary source beats polished paragraph, always.
- Exact-phrase-in-quotes is the twenty-second tool that catches most
  fabrications.
- Rerun the arithmetic yourself — always, non-negotiably.
- The screen beats the described screen.
- Three independent sources earns "safe to repeat in public."
- Sourcing is a *bridge* skill, not a scavenger hunt: two minutes,
  one tab, one decision.
